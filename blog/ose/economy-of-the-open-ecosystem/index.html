The Economy of the Open Ecosystem

This essay is the appendix to Part 2 of this thread, NVIDIA and the Open Stack. It is an optional read on the flow of investments and the current ecosystem, for a reader who comes to the thread without the economics. Its data are as of early November 2026 unless a later date is given. The essay follows the investment. The open software ecosystem is shaped less by principle than by where the liquidity of the AI buildout comes from, how long it lasts, and what the companies that supply it want in return. We trace that liquidity from the large platforms and the maker of the processors to the model laboratories, the open-model hub and the neoclouds, and we ask how the incentives of each large company decide which layers stay open. The macroeconomy enters only as the condition that sets the term of the investment, and closed and open models enter as the two destinations it flows to. We conclude that each plausible path raises the value of verification, and that its evidence belongs in the open layers.

This essay is the appendix to Part 2 of a thread on the open software ecosystem, and it examines the economics that decide which of its layers remain open. Part 2, NVIDIA and the Open Stack, examines the open ecosystem that NVIDIA is building as the market leader. This appendix is an optional read for a reader who comes to that essay without the economics: it describes the flow of investments and the current ecosystem. The first part, published in August 2026, asked which layers of an AI-native software stack must be open for an agent's work to be checked from outside the vendor, and it placed chips and energy outside its scope because they decide the cost floor rather than the correctness of software. The cost floor, however, decides how much software is produced, and the quantity of software decides how much must be verified. As such, this appendix follows the investment below the boundary that the first part drew and asks what it implies for the layers above it.

We approach the subject as engineers rather than as economists, and we use economic data only where it bears on that question. The macroeconomic series come from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis as published in early November 2026, and the figures for the AI industry come from company statements and the analysts we name. The series of Figure F22 were retrieved in mid November 2026. Each chart is drawn by a script from a data file kept with this essay. [1]

1. Introduction

Three figures describe the scale of the subject in November 2026. The four largest cloud and platform companies guided to capital expenditure of about $720 billion to $740 billion for 2026, by the estimates of trackers that compile their guidance, compared with more than $416 billion spent in 2025. [2] David Cahn of Sequoia estimates that one year of such spending requires $1.5 trillion of lifetime revenue from end customers. [3] Google reported processing more than 3.2 quadrillion tokens per month in May 2026, about 330 times its volume two years earlier. [4] These figures describe an industry that is spending and producing faster than any of its precedents, and they raise two questions that this thread cannot set aside: whether the spending will continue, and what it does to the amount of software that must be checked.

This essay is not about the midterm elections, not about whether AI is a bubble, which no one can predict, and not about the private lives of executives. It is about the flow of investments: the short-term liquidity that finances closed and open models, where it comes from, and how the incentives of the large companies that supply it make decisions for the open ecosystem. In the second half of November 2026 volatility rose sharply, because of the questions about the stability of NVIDIA and about the next move of the Federal Reserve. We assume that no further variable enters: the market returns to where it stood before Jensen Huang left NVIDIA, since he returned as its chief executive in November 2026, and we discuss the economic measures only. [5] We unpack those incentives one company at a time. The macroeconomic conditions of Section 2 matter only because they set the term and the price of that investment. Three challenges remain.

Challenge 1. The economic signal is mixed, and a conclusion that depends on the timing of the cycle cannot guide work planned over several years. Forecasts of a correction resembling those of 2001 or 2008 compete with an unemployment rate of 4.1%, and an oil shock that raised inflation to 4.2% in May 2026 competes with a record investment cycle. We therefore need the quantities that hold under each plausible path of the economy, not a forecast of which path will occur.

Challenge 2. Openness is a strategy that companies adopt where it raises the value of what they sell, so the layers that remain open depend on incentives rather than on principles. In September 2026, NVIDIA announced an agreement to acquire Hugging Face, the principal hub for open-weight models, for about $12.9 billion. [6] A layer that the first part classified as connective, and therefore required to be open, now has an acquirer whose business is selling the processors on which the models run. Predicting which layers stay open therefore requires following the investment that finances them.

Challenge 3. Cheaper generation multiplies the software that must be accepted. The price of a fixed level of capability fell about 200X in two years, and the volume of tokens that one large provider reports rose about 330X over a similar span. Every program among those tokens must be accepted, by a person or by a check, before it runs. We therefore need to know how the quantity of software that must be checked grows as the price of generating it falls.

In this work, we address these challenges by separating the conditions that change the timing of investment from the incentives that determine its direction. The key idea is a question that Jeff Bezos posed in 2012: what will not change over the next ten years, since a strategy can be built around what is stable in time. [7] Applied to this thread, the answer we propose is that no developer, customer or regulator will prefer less verification over the next three to five years, and the rest of the essay examines whether the evidence supports that answer.

The rest of this essay is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the macroeconomic conditions outside technology. Section 3 traces the flow of investments from the large platforms to the smaller companies that depend on them. Section 4 describes the private markets in which that investment is raised. Section 5 follows the investment through the AI buildout. Section 6 examines open and closed platforms as economic choices. Section 7 states what will not change and the threats to the validity of our reading, and Section 8 lists the next essays of the thread.

2. The current ecosystem outside technology

This section describes the economy in which the AI buildout is financed, in five parts: employment, prices, short and long interest rates, the templates by which crises are now handled, and the politics that bears on data centers. Its purpose is to establish which of these conditions could interrupt the buildout and which could only delay it.

Employment remains strong, and employment is the first condition that the Federal Reserve examines. The unemployment rate was 4.1% in October 2026, compared with 10.0% at the peak after the crisis of 2008 and 14.8% in April 2020. [1] Figure F1 shows the rate since 2000, with the three recessions of that period shaded. It is important to note that the law charges the Federal Reserve with maximum employment and stable prices, not with the level of asset prices. As such, a decline in technology stocks, such as the fall of the Nasdaq to 11% below its record in early November 2026, does not by itself call for a response. [8] Wall Street, in other words, is not Main Street from the perspective of the central bank.

Prices are the weaker condition, and their recent rise has an external cause that can be dated. After United States and Israeli strikes on Iran at the end of February 2026, an adviser to the commander of Iran's Revolutionary Guard announced the closure of the Strait of Hormuz in early March. [9] The price of West Texas Intermediate crude oil, which had averaged $64.50 per barrel in February, averaged $91 in March and about $100 in April and May. Consumer prices, which had risen 2.4% from a year earlier in February, rose 4.2% in May and 3.7% in September. Figure F2 places the two series on one time axis. Had oil remained near $65, inflation would presumably have continued toward the Federal Reserve's objective of 2%, since its rise began in the month that the price of oil rose. Instead, the Federal Open Market Committee raised its target range by 0.25 percentage point in September 2026, and the effective federal funds rate rose by that amount, its first increase since July 2023. [10, 1]

The Federal Reserve controls the short rate, but the long rate is set by the market's expectations of growth, inflation and risk. The federal funds rate is an overnight rate that the central bank administers directly. The yield on a 10-year Treasury bond, on the other hand, is the price at which investors lend to the government for a decade. It therefore reflects their expectations of growth and inflation over that decade and the premium they require for holding a long claim, and the yields on sovereign and corporate bonds add a further premium for the risk of default. Figure F3 shows how far the two rates can diverge. Between September 2024 and January 2025, the Federal Reserve cut its rate by 1.00 percentage point. Over the same months the 10-year yield rose from 3.63% in mid September 2024 to 4.79% in mid January 2025; as monthly averages, which are what the figure plots, it rose from 3.72% to 4.63%. In October 2026, the 10-year yield averaged 5.13% while the federal funds rate stood at 3.88%. The cost of the long-term capital that finances data centers is therefore set less by the central bank than by investors' view of growth and of the government's finances. The figure also shades in red the months in which the overnight rate stood above the 10-year yield. Such an inversion says that investors expect lower rates and weaker growth ahead, and it has usually come before a recession. By this measure the curve was inverted from December 2022 to December 2024, and no recession came. Our reading is that without the ChatGPT moment of November 2022 there was a very good chance of one, and the record of 2022 to 2024 below takes this up.

The response to a financial crisis now follows templates, which is a second difference from 2001 and 2008, the first being the strength of employment. In June 2017, Janet Yellen, then chair of the Federal Reserve, said that it "would be going too far" to say there would never be another financial crisis, and added: "I hope that it will not be in our lifetimes and I don't believe it will be". [11] The remark was widely mocked after 2020 and 2023. The other side of the record, however, is that each crisis since 2008 has been met with a faster version of the same response, which combines lower short rates, purchases of assets and emergency lending. Figure F4 shows the three responses on the Federal Reserve's balance sheet. Total assets rose from $0.91 trillion to $2.25 trillion between September and December 2008, from $4.24 trillion to $7.17 trillion between March and June 2020, and from $8.34 trillion to $8.73 trillion in the two weeks after Silicon Valley Bank failed in March 2023. [1] The recession of 2020 lasted two months, compared with eighteen months for that of 2008 and 2009. Figure F15 compares the lengths of the three recessions.

The record of 2022 to 2024 extends this evidence. Two events that have usually preceded a recession did not produce one this time: the failure of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023, at the time the second-largest bank failure in U.S. history, was contained within two weeks by a guarantee of its deposits and a new lending facility, and the inversion of the yield curve from July 2022 to early September 2024, the longest on record, ended without a recession. Neither did the rate rises from March 2022 to July 2023, which took the effective federal funds rate from 0.08% to 5.33%. [1] The Federal Reserve had the instruments to avert a recession, and the release of ChatGPT in November 2022 gave the markets a boost: the S&P 500 rose 24% in 2023, from 3,839.50 to 4,769.83 at the year ends. [12] Figure F15 adds the recession that did not come to the three that did. The financial instruments are, on this evidence, stronger than at any earlier point in the period. As such, a correction in the markets that finance the AI buildout is likely to be shortened by the same templates, even if it is not prevented.

The alternative to the templates is known, because two markets lived through it. Figure F18 sets the S&P 500 at its peak of August 2000 beside the Nikkei 225 at its peak of December 1989 and plots each against the years since. The S&P 500 touched its peak in 2007, fell by half again, and held above it only from 2013, a lost decade for an investor who bought at the top. The Nikkei fell to a fifth of its peak by 2009 and regained it only in 2024, after thirty-four years. The Bank of Japan began purchasing assets in March 2001, eleven years after the peak, and expanded the purchases only in April 2013; the Federal Reserve began within three months of the failure of Lehman Brothers. We do not claim that the timing of the purchases alone explains the difference, since the two economies differed in demography, banking and exchange rates. We do observe that the instruments now exist, have been used three times in eighteen years, and were used faster each time, so it is difficult to imagine a correction in the markets that finance AI being allowed to run for a decade.

The forecasters who were right in 2008 have been wrong since, and the reason is the same. A saying from the 1930s holds that one can take a man out of the Depression but not the Depression out of the man. Jeremy Grantham and Michael Burry anticipated the crisis of 2008, when the game was the one they knew, and both have forecast further crashes repeatedly since 2010, during which the S&P 500 rose more than 4X. We read their record not as a failure of analysis but as evidence that the game changed in 2008. A market in which the central bank will buy assets and guarantee deposits within weeks does not behave as the market of 1929 or 2008 did, and a method calibrated on the earlier market will keep predicting the earlier market's crashes. Chancellor and Alden describe the cost of the new game.

The templates have critics, and we note two whose arguments bear on this thread. Edward Chancellor argues in The Price of Time that interest is the price of time, and that holding it artificially low misallocates capital, inflates the prices of assets and keeps unproductive companies alive. [13] Lyn Alden argues in Broken Money that a government with large debts enters a regime in which its deficits, rather than its central bank, drive the growth of money. [14] Both arguments imply that the templates have a cost, which is paid in the long rate and in the allocation of capital rather than in the depth of the next recession.

Whether the United States can grow out of its debt depends on the difference between nominal growth and the interest rate. Figure F12 shows the two ratios that frame the question: federal debt was 121% of GDP in the second quarter of 2026, up from 55% in 2000, and federal interest payments reached 4.0% of GDP in the third quarter, compared with 2.5% in early 2022. [1] A ratio of debt to output falls when output grows faster than the debt, which occurs when nominal growth exceeds the interest rate on the debt and the deficit before interest is small. Figure F5 compares nominal growth with the 10-year yield, which approximates the cost of new federal borrowing. Nominal growth exceeded the yield in 39 of the 40 quarters of the 2010s and in all 23 quarters since 2021, and in the third quarter of 2026 it was 5.9% against 4.8%. The condition for growing out of the debt therefore holds today. A physical buildout has carried such growth twice before, with the railways in the nineteenth century and after the Second World War in the 1950s, and the physical buildout of AI would be a third American industrial revolution. That is the optimism of the market, and it is why interest rates may not return to the level of the 2010s. However, a long rate that rises toward the rate of growth, as it did in October 2026, narrows the margin, which is the risk that Chancellor and Alden describe.

The precedent for growing out of a debt of this size is the United States after the Second World War, and Alden's argument is that the precedent cannot be repeated in the same way. Federal debt held by the public was 106% of GDP in 1946 and 23% in 1974. [15] It fell not because the debt was repaid but because nominal output grew faster than the interest paid on it. The Federal Reserve held long-term Treasury yields at or below 2.5% until its accord with the Treasury in 1951, inflation ran ahead of those yields in several years, and real output grew at a pace that the post-war labour force and the reconstruction of trading partners allowed. Broken Money calls this arrangement financial repression and observes that the holders of the debt, who were largely domestic savers, paid for it through yields below inflation. [14]

Three conditions differ today. The deficit before interest is large in an expansion rather than closing as the war ended, so the debt grows even when the growth condition holds. The debt is held in larger part by foreign official holders and by funds that can sell, which limits how far yields can be held below inflation. Finally, the rate of real growth that the post-war decades reached is not available to an economy with slower growth of its labour force, unless the productivity gains that the AI buildout promises arrive. As such, the question of this essay returns in the fiscal arithmetic: whether the software now being produced raises output enough to carry the debt that financed it.

The two periods do not match, however. From 1946 to 1974 the buildout was physical: highways, power and suburbs, financed with public debt and repressed yields. From 2000 to 2026 the growth was mostly digital, in software and services, and only in the 2020s have data centers and power been financed at scale, with corporate debt and vendor credit. A physical buildout is where a real buildout can take place, and with the building of data centers and of energy, American industry looks to be at the start of one. This is our reading, between the lines, of Ray Dalio's Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order and of Alden's Broken Money. [14, 38]

Figure F22 sets the two periods on one scale, with the physical buildout of 1946 to 1974 in the first column and the mostly digital years of 2000 to 2026 in the second. It counts debt held by the public, which was 98% of GDP in 2025; the total debt of Figure F12 also counts what federal accounts hold. After 1946 the debt rose in dollars, from $242 billion to $344 billion in 1974, while it fell as a share of GDP. The figure gives the two rates: nominal GDP grew 7.0% a year from 1947 to 1974, against a long Treasury yield that averaged 4.2%. That yield was below inflation in 5 of the 29 years from 1946 to 1974, namely 1946, 1947, 1948, 1951 and 1974. Private nonresidential investment was 10.2% of GDP in 1955 and about 14% in 2025. Government investment, which includes defense, was 5.7% of GDP in 1955, peaked at 7.3% in 1961 and was 3.6% in 2025. The figure uses the FRED series, which read 106.3% and 22.2% for 1946 and 1974 where the OMB table reads 106.1% and 23.2%. [1]

Politics affects the buildout through policy on data centers, and the midterm elections changed that policy in several of the largest markets. Democratic candidates won the governorships of Texas, Ohio, Georgia, Nevada and Iowa, and the winners in Texas and Ohio campaigned on restricting new data centers. [8] Morgan Stanley called the result in Texas, a state that JLL expects to overtake northern Virginia as the largest data-center market by 2030, the most important election result for data-center development. It warned that the results could split the country into areas that are open to new data centers and areas that are not. Local referendums approved restrictions in 44 of the 45 contests counted, and about three-quarters of voters opposed a new data center in their own community, with higher support in Wyoming, West Virginia, Louisiana and Mississippi. [8] We expect the market to adapt as it has before, by building where power, water and permits remain available, and we therefore read the election as a change in the location and cost of the buildout rather than in its direction.

Figure F17 gathers the series of this section on one time axis, with the events that this essay refers to marked on it. Read from left to right, the three recessions show the same order of events: the stock index falls first, the central bank cuts its rate, and unemployment peaks last and recovers slowest, which took six years after 2009 and two after 2020. In 2026 the index is near its record and the policy rate is rising, a combination that preceded neither of the earlier recessions.

In summary, the conditions outside technology can delay the buildout through the long rate and through the politics of power and water, but none of them, on the evidence of 2026, reverses it. Next, we follow the investment inside the industry.

3. From big tech to small tech: the flow of investments

This section describes, at a high level, how investment moves from the four large platforms and the maker of the processors to the smaller companies of the AI economy, by which we mean the model laboratories OpenAI and Anthropic, the open-model hub Hugging Face, and the neoclouds that rent out processors. Its purpose is to show that the small companies are financed by the large ones, and that the investment often returns to its source as a purchase commitment.

Each large company finances the small ones from a business that predates AI. Microsoft funds its investments from cloud and software, Amazon from cloud and retail, Google from advertising, and NVIDIA from selling the processors that every recipient must buy. Meta also funds its models from advertising. It finances none of the model laboratories; its large outside investment, made in June 2025, is in Scale AI, a supplier of training data, and it releases model weights instead. [39] Figure F16 draws the announced flows. Microsoft holds about 27% of OpenAI, which in turn committed in October 2025 to buy $250 billion of Azure services. [16] Amazon invested $8 billion in Anthropic, Google invested in Anthropic and agreed to supply up to one million TPUs, and in November 2025 Microsoft and NVIDIA committed up to $5 billion and $10 billion respectively, while Anthropic committed to buy $30 billion of Azure capacity. [17] NVIDIA signed a letter of intent in September 2025 to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI as its systems are deployed; in February 2026 it invested $30 billion in OpenAI's funding round, which is reported to have replaced that letter. [37] Under an arrangement reported in early July 2026, it agrees to rent back unused capacity from neoclouds at a fixed rate. [18, 19, 20]

NVIDIA also funds the open ecosystem directly. It agreed in September 2026 to acquire Hugging Face for $12.93 billion. [6] In August 2026, it reportedly agreed to license Poolside's Model Factory, the system that Poolside used to build its models, for $6 billion on a non-exclusive basis, and to invest $1 billion in the company. It has also invested in Reflection AI, in an amount that we have not confirmed. Poolside released the weights of its Laguna XS.2 model in April 2026, and Reflection AI announced Beam, its first open-weight model, in October 2026. [21] The incentive is simple. NVIDIA earns more when the whole pie grows, whichever company takes the slice, and every open model, open runtime and open hub lowers the cost of entering the market that buys its processors; NVIDIA is therefore trying to make the pie bigger, and the open ecosystem is the cheapest way to do it.

The investment is circular in two senses, and the circularity is the design of the structure rather than a flaw in it. First, an investment in a model laboratory returns to the investor as a purchase of its cloud or its processors. For example, a large part of what Microsoft invested in OpenAI comes back as the $250 billion of Azure revenue that OpenAI committed to, and NVIDIA's letter of intent for OpenAI tied each step of the investment to the deployment of the systems that OpenAI buys from NVIDIA. Second, the recipients are among the largest customers of the funders, so the funders' growth depends on the investment they have themselves supplied. Table 1 summarizes each flow and what returns. We note that a circular flow is how a vendor finances the adoption of its platform, and the equipment makers of the telecommunications buildout of the 1990s financed their customers in the same way. It does, however, mean that a fall in the small companies' revenue would appear on the large companies' books twice, as lost sales and as impaired investments.

By September 2026, The Economist estimated that NVIDIA had pledged more than $70 billion of investment in startups over three years and had offered about $300 billion of financial support to its customers, a contingent exposure that includes guarantees of power purchases and of residual values, and it called NVIDIA the central bank of AI. [22] Gavin Baker had described NVIDIA as a possible Federal Reserve of AI about two weeks earlier, in August. [23] The comparison is accurate in one respect and misleading in another. Like a central bank, NVIDIA supplies the scarce input of the system, influences how much of it each participant receives, and provides financing when private lenders hesitate. Unlike a central bank, it profits from the demand that it finances, and The Economist notes that the longer the boom lasts, the larger the losses it will bear if demand falls. [22] Next, we describe the markets in which this investment is raised.

4. Private markets: where the liquidity is raised

This section describes the markets in which the small companies of Section 3 raise their investment. Its purpose is to explain why liquidity has been easy to raise without a public listing, and what that ease does to the discipline that the open ecosystem would otherwise receive from public markets.

The largest AI companies raised their capital while they were private, and the private markets have supplied them with sums that only public markets could supply a decade ago. Figure F21 lists five financings of 2025. OpenAI raised $40 billion in a primary round led by SoftBank in March 2025 at a valuation of $300 billion, and its employees sold about $6.6 billion of shares in October 2025 at a valuation of $500 billion. Anthropic raised $13 billion in September 2025 at $183 billion, and xAI raised about $10 billion of debt and equity in July 2025. Meta financed its Hyperion data center with about $27 billion arranged with Blue Owl in October 2025, in a structure that keeps most of the debt off Meta's own balance sheet. [24] None of these required a prospectus, a quarterly report or a public float. Three were sales of equity, one combined debt with equity, and one, the second largest, was credit arranged with a private lender.

One of these companies has since listed. In November 2026 Anthropic set the terms of a public listing on Nasdaq, 825 million shares at $70 to $78. It then priced the offering at $83 and sold 980 million shares, which became 1.127 billion when the underwriters took up their option for a further 147 million. Trading began later that month under the ticker ANT, with a first trade at $111.50 and a close of $97.17, and by late November the price had fallen to a close of $82, below the offering price. In a separate private placement, seven investors that include Google, NVIDIA, AMD and Microsoft buy $26 billion of shares. [25, 41]

Three mechanisms make this liquidity easy to raise. First, private equity and private credit funds grew through the decade of near-zero rates described in Section 2, and they now hold capital that must be deployed; private credit alone was estimated by Preqin at about $1.7 trillion of assets as of June 2023. [24] Second, the sovereign and strategic investors that lead these rounds, including SoftBank, the Gulf funds and the large platforms themselves, invest for position rather than for a near-term return, so the price of the round is set by the last willing buyer rather than by a market. Third, the vendor financing of Section 3 means that a round need not be fully funded in cash: a commitment of processors, cloud capacity or a purchase guarantee counts toward the headline figure. As such, a model laboratory can raise the equivalent of a large public offering in weeks, repeatedly, without the disclosure that a public offering would require.

The cost of easy liquidity is the absence of the discipline that a public market imposes, and that cost falls on the open ecosystem. A public company must report what it earns, what it owes and what it has committed to buy, and the market prices those reports every day. A private laboratory reports to its investors, and its investors are often also its suppliers and customers. The circularity of Section 3 is therefore invisible from outside until a lender, such as the bond market that priced CoreWeave's debt in November 2026, demands a price for it. We note that the open layers, which exist to let independent parties check one another's work, have no equivalent in the financial layer: there is no portable, independently checkable record of who has committed what to whom. Part 4 examines whether such a record could exist. Next, we follow the investment through the revenue it must earn and the debt it has created.

5. Following the investment in AI

This section examines the revenue that the buildout requires, the effect of falling prices on the quantity of computation, and the debt that finances the neoclouds. Its purpose is to establish what determines the quantity of software that will need to be verified.

The spending is concentrated in four companies, each of which is increasing it. Figure F11 compares what Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta spent in 2025 with the midpoint of their guidance for 2026. Each raised its spending by between 44% and 119%, and Amazon alone guided to about $220 billion. [2] The question that follows is what revenue this spending requires.

The revenue question has been restated four times, and its size has grown at each restatement. David Cahn's method takes NVIDIA's projected run-rate revenue from data centers, doubles it because processors are about half the cost of a data center, and doubles it again because the end customer of the processors needs a gross margin of about 50%. [3] The result was $200 billion in September 2023, $600 billion in June 2024, $840 billion in June 2025 and $1.5 trillion in July 2026, a figure that Cahn confirms by doubling the roughly $750 billion of data-center capital expenditure expected in 2026. Figure F6 shows the four restatements. In October 2026, Cahn attributed the sell-off in AI stocks to "expectations that got ahead of themselves, rather than any fundamental deterioration". [3] We conclude that the question remains open. The revenue that answers it, however, will be earned largely by producing software, and in particular code, which brings the question to this thread.

As computation becomes cheaper, the total quantity consumed rises faster than the price falls. William Stanley Jevons observed in 1865 that more efficient steam engines increased Britain's consumption of coal rather than reducing it, because cheaper power found new uses. [26] Satya Nadella invoked the observation in January 2025, after DeepSeek released its efficient models, to predict that the use of AI would rise as its cost fell. [27] Figure F7 shows both halves of the observation for language models. According to Epoch AI, the lowest price per million tokens for a model that matches the March 2023 version of GPT-4 on the MMLU benchmark fell from $37.50 to $0.18 by February 2025, about 200X in two years. The rate of decline ranged from 9X to 900X per year depending on the task and the level of performance. [28] Over a similar span, from May 2024 to May 2026, the number of tokens that Google reported processing per month rose from 9.7 trillion to more than 3.2 quadrillion, about 330X. [4]

The two series measure different things, and a token is not a line of code. Nevertheless, some of those tokens are spent by coding agents producing programs, and every such program must be accepted, by a person or by a check, before it runs. Thus, a fall in the price of generation increases the volume of software that must be verified.

The weakest link in this financing is the debt of the neoclouds, the companies that buy processors with debt and rent them out. CoreWeave, the largest of them, reported total debt of $35 billion at the end of June 2026, up from $22.7 billion of long-term debt three months earlier, against a quarterly capital expenditure of $9.4 billion and a contracted backlog of $104 billion. [19] Much of that debt consists of loans secured on the processors themselves, at loan-to-value ratios that analysts place between 60% and 70%, and the collateral depreciates over a period that we take to be three to six years, an assumption of ours that the cited articles do not state, while the loans mature between 2026 and 2028. In early November 2026, the yield on CoreWeave's bond due 2032 exceeded 16%, and the cost of insuring its debt for five years rose as high as about 1,289 basis points on that day; the article that we cite sets about 1,100 for CoreWeave beside about 139 for NVIDIA and 298 for Oracle, as Figure F13 shows. [19]

NVIDIA's financing reaches this layer too: under the arrangement reported in early July 2026, it agrees to rent back unused capacity from neocloud partners at a fixed rate. [20] We note that a lender who holds a processor as collateral holds an asset whose value depends on the demand for the software that runs on it, which is the demand that this section has traced to the price and volume of tokens. The neocloud balance sheet is therefore where a change in that demand would first be observed, and Part 4 examines it in detail.

6. Open and closed as economic choices

This section examines three precedents in which open and closed platforms coexisted, and applies them to NVIDIA's investments in open software. Its purpose is to state which layers the incentives will keep open.

In each precedent, the open platform won the volume and the closed platform kept the margin. Microsoft's chief executive called Linux "a cancer" in 2001, his successor announced in 2014 that "Microsoft loves Linux", and by 2018 about half of the virtual machines on Microsoft's cloud ran Linux. [29] Microsoft did not open Windows; instead, it adopted the open layer through which its cloud reaches customers and competed on the services above that layer. In smartphones, Android accounted for about 75% of smartphone sales in the second quarter of 2026, while Apple, with 18% of the units shipped in 2022, earned 85% of the industry's operating profit, as Figure F9 shows. [30] In an earlier paper, we observed that over 90% of mobile malware targeted Android, which had at least 1.4 billion deployed devices at the beginning of 2017, a consequence of its openness and popularity, and that vetting apps at that volume required analyses fast enough to run when an app is submitted for distribution. [31] The open platform, in other words, carried both the volume and the burden of checking it.

Open-weight models follow the same pattern, with a different geography, and in 2026 the volume flipped. Figure F19 shows the share of tokens routed through OpenRouter, a marketplace that serves both kinds of model, that was served by open-weight models. The share was about a quarter through 2025, more than half for the first time in February 2026, and 65% to 75% from June to October 2026, by which time open-weight models served 476 trillion tokens a month against 260 trillion for closed ones. [32] Within the open tokens, the share served by Chinese models rose from under half in early 2025 to 91% in October 2026. Figure F20 shows the same geography in downloads from Hugging Face, where Chinese labs passed U.S. labs during 2025 and served 266 million downloads in October 2026 against 109 million. [32]

It is important to note what a token measures. It is a unit of volume, not of capability: a token from a frontier closed model and a token from a small open model are priced differently and do different work, so the flip in volume is a flip in where the routine work is done, not in where the frontier is. The closed frontier models, like iOS, earn most of the revenue, whereas the open models, like Android, now carry most of the volume and are where most derivatives, fine-tuned variants and local deployments are built.

NVIDIA's investments in open software follow from a rule that Joel Spolsky stated in 2002: a company gains when the complements of its product become cheap. [33] Every open model, open runtime and open compiler that runs well on NVIDIA's processors increases the demand for them. NVIDIA has accordingly released open models (Nemotron), an open framework for serving inference (Dynamo), open foundation models for robots (GR00T) and Rust programming models for its GPUs (CUDA Rust), and it agreed to acquire Hugging Face in September 2026. NVIDIA states that its compute will not be required to build on or deploy through Hugging Face and that the platform will continue to support several clouds and accelerators. [34, 6] Jensen Huang wrote that Hugging Face would remain an open platform for the whole ecosystem; these are NVIDIA's commitments, stated in its own announcement. [6]

The first part of this thread proposed a rule for this situation: a layer that exists to connect independent systems must be open, and a leaf may be closed provided that the layers around it emit portable evidence. Hugging Face is a connective layer by that rule, since its value comes from connecting model builders, users and processors that do not share an owner. Its openness after the acquisition, however, is a commitment of its new owner rather than a property of its design. We therefore expect the incentive to keep it open to hold for as long as open models increase the demand for NVIDIA's processors, and we note that users will want to check such a commitment through open formats, reproducible downloads and records that do not depend on one vendor's dashboard. No closing of the acquisition had been reported by late November 2026.

7. What will not change

This section states the conclusions that hold under each path of the economy described in Section 2, and the threats to the validity of our reading.

We find two quantities that do not depend on the timing of the cycle. First, the price of generating code falls and its volume rises: the price of a fixed level of capability fell about 200X in two years while the volume of tokens rose about 330X, and a slower buildout would slow the fall in price without reversing it. Second, the incentive of NVIDIA is to keep the connective layers open, because open models and runtimes increase the demand for its processors. Figure F14 places these conclusions on the stack: investment flows downward from software to energy, and the evidence that accepts a program must flow upward, from the layer where it ran to the record that admits it. The body of this essay establishes the first quantity from prices and volumes and the second from NVIDIA's incentive, and it does not measure what developers, customers or regulators prefer. We therefore state our conclusion as an expectation that follows from the two quantities: over the next three to five years no developer, customer or regulator will prefer less verification, and the evidence that verification produces will need to cross the boundaries between vendors, which is possible only in open layers.

Our reading is subject to three threats to validity. Regarding external validity, the macroeconomic data describe the United States, and the conclusions may not transfer to other economies. Regarding internal validity, several figures are company guidance or secondary reports, including the capital expenditure for 2026, Google's token volumes and The Economist's estimates of NVIDIA's financing, and we have named the source of each. The FRED series are the vintage of November 2026 and may be revised. Regarding construct validity, a token is not a line of code, capital expenditure is not demand, and Apple's share of profit dates from 2022. Each measure indicates a direction, and none measures the quantity of software that is verified, which no public source reports.
