NVIDIA and the Open Stack

NVIDIA is the market leader in processors for AI, and it is building or financing an open version of nearly every layer above them. For each layer we state the incentive of NVIDIA to open it. It is by design that NVIDIA opens these layers.

NVIDIA sells the processors for AI, and it is also investing in an open version of nearly every layer of software above them. It has released open models, a framework for serving inference, a runtime for agents and a programming model for Rust; it has financed laboratories that train open models, and it has agreed to acquire Hugging Face, the principal hub on which such models are distributed. The openness of these layers is, however, not of one kind. Some are open by licence and by design, so that no later decision of the owner can close them, whereas others are open only because the owner has said so. As such, this essay states for each layer the incentive of NVIDIA to open it and what holds it open.

This essay is Part 2 of a thread on the open software ecosystem. Part 1 asked which layers of an AI-native stack must be open for an agent's work to be checked from outside the vendor. This part examines the open stack that NVIDIA is building above its own hardware. An appendix, The Economy of the Open Ecosystem, is optional reading on the macroeconomy and the flow of investment in the AI buildout.

Two things this essay does not cover. The first is the departure of Jensen Huang as NVIDIA's chief executive and his return, both in November 2026, a blip. The second is what The Economist has already covered in Nvidia is the central bank of AI, the financing that NVIDIA extends to startups and to its customers, which we recommend reading beside this essay. [1]

1. Introduction

Three facts from 2026 give the scale. In September, NVIDIA announced an agreement to acquire Hugging Face, the principal hub for open-weight models. [2] In August, it reportedly agreed to license, for $6 billion, the system that builds Poolside's open-weight models for code. [3] In October, open-weight models served 476 trillion of the tokens routed through OpenRouter, against 260 trillion for closed models. [4]

Challenge 1. The market leader funds the open layers. NVIDIA sells the processors, and it also releases open models, finances laboratories that train them, and has agreed to buy the hub that distributes them. An open ecosystem with one sponsor depends on that sponsor's incentive. We need to state the incentive of NVIDIA at each layer.

Challenge 2. Openness is held either by the design or by a promise. A permissive licence, weights that anyone can download and a foundation that holds a project all outlast a change of sponsor. A statement of intent may not. We need to classify each layer by what holds it open.

We address these challenges with two questions for every layer: who benefits when it is open, and what would have to happen for it to close. The key idea is that the first answer explains the funding and the second the durability, and that the two differ by layer.

2. The stack, layer by layer

This section describes what NVIDIA has opened, bought or financed above its processors. Figure F1 lists the layers with the artifact in each, its publisher, its licence or home, and the incentive of NVIDIA to open it.

Open models: Nemotron and GR00T. NVIDIA announced the Nemotron 3 family of language models in December 2025. The cards of Nemotron 3 Ultra, released in June 2026, and of Nemotron 3.5 Lightning carry the OpenMDW 1.1 licence, which permits use "without restriction". [5] Older Nemotron models carry the NVIDIA Open Model License, whose conditions Section 4 examines. Isaac GR00T, a foundation model for humanoid robots announced in March 2025, has its code under Apache 2.0 and the weights of its current version, N1.7, under the NVIDIA Open Model License. [6]

The hub: Hugging Face. NVIDIA's own page on the hub listed more than a thousand models in November 2026. [5] The acquisition had not closed by late November, and on its results call that month NVIDIA described it as a definitive agreement entered into in September. [7] It is expected to close in the first half of 2027, and the Department of Justice is reported to be examining it. [8]

Model building: Poolside. Poolside trains models that write code, and in April 2026 it released the weights of Laguna XS.2 under Apache 2.0. In August 2026, NVIDIA reportedly agreed to license the Model Factory, the system that builds those models, for $6 billion on a non-exclusive basis, and to invest $1 billion. [3, 9] We found no announcement by either company. The Model Factory itself is not open: the licence is a private contract.

Laboratories: Reflection AI and Thinking Machines Lab. Reflection AI raised $2 billion in October 2025, in a round in which NVIDIA invested and which it is reported to have led, although the article that we cite lists NVIDIA among the investors without naming a lead. It released the weights of Beam, its first open-weight model, under Apache 2.0 in October 2026, after a training run on 10,500 NVIDIA GB300 GPUs. [10] Thinking Machines Lab announced in March 2026 a partnership to deploy at least one gigawatt of NVIDIA's Vera Rubin systems, with an undisclosed investment by NVIDIA, to "broaden access to frontier AI and open models". [11]

Inference serving: Dynamo. Dynamo, unveiled in March 2025 under Apache 2.0, serves inference across many machines. [12] It runs no model itself and coordinates the engines that do, among them the open vLLM and SGLang and NVIDIA's own TensorRT-LLM. Its documentation names AMD and Intel processors as supported, while its support matrix lists NVIDIA processors only.

Agent runtime: OpenShell. OpenShell is a sandboxed runtime for autonomous agents, governed by policies written in YAML and licensed under Apache 2.0. [13] It was marked alpha when we first read it in September 2026, and the mark was removed later that month. It runs without NVIDIA hardware: it needs a container or a virtual machine, and a GPU is optional. The Cloud Native Computing Foundation's vote to accept it as a Sandbox project passed, and the application closed in October 2026. NVIDIA announced the contribution in November. By late November the repository had moved from NVIDIA's organisation on GitHub to that of the project at the foundation, while the foundation's onboarding list was still open. The move does not yet change who maintains the code: in November the maintainers' file listed fourteen people, eleven from NVIDIA and three from Red Hat. Also in November, the project merged native support for Cedar, an open policy language with a formal model in Lean, as a second language for the policies of a sandbox. The support is in development builds only, behind an experimental flag. [13]

Kernel programming in Rust: CUDA Rust. CUDA Rust, introduced in September 2026 under Apache 2.0, gives Rust programmers two ways to write GPU kernels. [14] The first, cuda-oxide, is a backend for the Rust compiler in early alpha. The second, cuTile Rust, embeds the kernel's source text in the program and lowers it at run time to a bytecode called Tile IR. A closed program from the CUDA toolkit, tileiras, compiles that bytecode for the GPU.

An inference engine in Rust: grout. Hugging Face publishes grout, an engine written in Rust that serves the Qwen3 models, with every GPU kernel written in cuTile Rust. [15] NVIDIA's README for cuTile Rust says that grout was built "in collaboration with Hugging Face", and the first commit of grout was made in March 2026, more than five months before the acquisition was announced. The hub's own engine thus already sat on NVIDIA's kernel layer, and neither runs without an NVIDIA GPU.

Figure F2 places these moves in time, and Figure F3 sets the investments on one scale. Hugging Face and Poolside add to $19.93 billion, about two thirds of the $30 billion that NVIDIA invested in OpenAI's funding round of February 2026. [26] That investment is reported to have replaced the letter of intent of September 2025 for up to $100 billion, and the appendix lists it beside up to $10 billion for Anthropic. In November 2026 there was another transaction with a closed laboratory. Anthropic, whose shares began trading on Nasdaq that month, set terms that include a private placement of $26 billion with seven investors, among them NVIDIA, with a reported share of $10 billion. [16]

3. Why NVIDIA invests in the open layers

A company gains when the complements of its product become cheap. Joel Spolsky stated the rule in 2002: demand for a product rises when the prices of its complements fall. [17] Models, hubs, serving software, runtimes and programming models are the complements of a processor. Figure F4 draws the mechanism: cheap open layers widen the base of builders, who run more training and inference and buy more processors.

The incentive differs by layer, and Figure F1 names it for each. Open models give every buyer of a processor something to run, and the card of Nemotron 3 Ultra states its minimum hardware in NVIDIA systems only. [5] The hub is where models are found, and the Model Factory and the laboratories add teams that can train. Dynamo raises the inference served per processor, OpenShell gives an enterprise agents that it can govern, and CUDA Rust adds a community of programmers whose kernels stay on the CUDA toolkit.

The second incentive is a wider base of customers. The platforms behind the closed laboratories design processors of their own. The appendix lists two cases: Google supplies Anthropic with up to one million of its TPUs, and Amazon's investment in Anthropic came with the adoption of its Trainium processors. An open model has no tie to a platform. Whoever downloads the weights chooses the hardware, and the serving and kernel layers of Section 2 run on NVIDIA's. Many small buyers are therefore a steadier market than what Nathan Lambert calls "a few customers who would develop their own chips". [18]

Open models already carry most of the routed volume. Figure F5 shows the share of tokens routed through OpenRouter that open-weight models served: 26% in January 2025, above half for the first time in February 2026, and 65% in October 2026 after a peak of 75% in July. [4] A token is a unit of volume and not of capability. We read the volume as the routine work, which is what fills processors, although the counts do not say which tasks were routine or how much processor time each took. Chinese laboratories served 91% of the open tokens in October 2026, and TechCrunch reads NVIDIA's stakes in the two laboratories as support for American open models. [9]

These shares come from the Open Models Dashboard of Interconnects, which is updated daily and also counts the downloads and the derivatives of open models on Hugging Face. Figure F6 is a screenshot of its first page, which shows downloads from Hugging Face, a different measure from the tokens of Figure F5. Nathan Lambert, who writes Interconnects, has also written there on NVIDIA's support for open models. [18] If you are in the open ecosystem, please follow his Substack, Interconnects.

4. Promise or property

This section classifies each layer by what holds it open. A property of the design is something that a third party can use without the owner's consent: a permissive licence on code that can be forked, weights that can be downloaded and kept, or a foundation that holds the project. A promise is a statement of what the owner intends. Figure F7 answers three questions for each layer and states what holds it open.

The software is open by a property of the design. Dynamo, OpenShell, CUDA Rust and grout are under Apache 2.0, so anyone may copy and change them whatever NVIDIA later decides. The newest Nemotron weights and the weights of Laguna XS.2 and Beam belong to the same class, since a downloaded file under a permissive licence cannot be recalled. No layer is open on every question. OpenShell comes closest: a foundation has voted to take it and now holds its repository, although eleven of its fourteen maintainers are NVIDIA's and the onboarding is not finished.

Some weights are open under a condition. The NVIDIA Open Model License covers older Nemotron models and the weights of GR00T N1.7. It ends for a user who bypasses a safety guardrail of the model without putting a comparable one in its place, and for a user who brings copyright or patent litigation alleging that the model infringes. [5, 6] The second condition is a common one, and OpenMDW 1.1 carries a similar clause; the first is what distinguishes this licence. Both are terms of a licence and not promises, but under the first the grant can end because of how a user runs the model.

The layers nearest the processor rest on a closed base. The CUDA toolkit is licensed "only for use in systems with NVIDIA GPUs". [19] The engine grout shows the consequence in one program. Its kernels are Rust source that anyone can read under Apache 2.0. One step of its path, the compiler tileiras, is a closed binary from the toolkit, as are the cuBLAS library that it calls and the driver that loads the result. [14, 15] The program is open by licence and closed at the steps that decide which hardware it runs on.

The hub is open by a promise. The announcement of September 2026 is written in the first person by Jensen Huang. It states that Hugging Face "will remain an open platform for the entire AI ecosystem" and that "NVIDIA compute will not be required to build on or deploy through Hugging Face". [2] No licence, format or foundation carries either sentence, and we found no statement by Hugging Face on its governance after the acquisition. The hub connects model builders, users and processors that do not share an owner. The connective layer of the stack is therefore the one held by a promise.

5. A bigger pie, not fully circular finance (Ponzi scheme)

This section states how we read NVIDIA's investments as a whole: three points on the bubble, the circularity and the role of a market leader, an observation from academia, and the precedent we find closest. Figure F8 draws the second point, the difference between a circular flow that produces nothing and an investment made ahead of demand.

Every bubble is said to be different this time, and even if this one bursts, the instruments to handle it exist. Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff named the belief in their history of eight centuries of financial crises, This Time Is Different. [20] We do not argue that this time is different. We argue that the Federal Reserve now has instruments for a burst, used and refined after the crisis of 2008, the pandemic of 2020 and the rate rises of 2022, when it contained the bank failures that began with Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) in March 2023 within two weeks with a new lending facility. The appendix sets out that record, and it also states its limit: the instruments shorten a correction and do not prevent one, and the central bank answers for employment and prices, not for the level of technology stocks.

A Ponzi scheme creates no value; capitalism with fractional reserve banking does. A Ponzi scheme returns the deposits of later investors to earlier ones, and it ends when the deposits stop, because nothing was produced. A bank that lends more than it holds in reserve creates credit, and that credit is sound when what it finances produces more than it costs. NVIDIA's investments in its customers come back as purchases of its processors, which can look like a circular flow. What separates the two is whether the processors produce something that their buyers can sell: models, software and services. Figure F8 sets the two readings side by side. The appendix gives the size of the test: by David Cahn's estimate, one year of the present spending requires $1.5 trillion of revenue from end customers, and the appendix concludes that the question of whether that revenue arrives remains open. As such, our reading is a claim about the kind of flow and not a forecast of its outcome.

NVIDIA's growth has been extraordinary, and the only way to sustain it is to grow the pie for everyone. The limit that NVIDIA meets is not the limit of a company but that of the technology ecosystem it leads: it cannot sell more processors than the industry can put to use. A market leader is therefore responsible for taking the whole industry forward, which is the lesson of Intel at its peak. NVIDIA invests on both sides of the line, in the closed laboratories, OpenAI and Anthropic, and in the open layers of Section 2, so that there are more builders on each side. It may look like a circular financing, but its philosophy is that of fractional reserve: invest ahead of the demand, in the expectation that what is built repays the investment.

In academia, the supercomputing ecosystem from HPC to AI is NVIDIA's. Most of the cluster computing labs that we know run on NVIDIA's processors, although AMD and Cerebras are the names most often given as alternatives. For the largest systems, the TOP500 list of November 2026 shows the same: more than half of its systems with accelerators use NVIDIA's chips, against about a tenth for AMD's, although El Capitan and Frontier, two of the largest, use AMD's. The list mixes the systems of industry, national laboratories and universities, so it does not show the share in academic laboratories alone. [27] The National Research Platform (NRP), which I know from my Ph.D., is an example: the GPU models that its documentation lists are all NVIDIA's, from the GeForce GTX 1070 to the GH200 Grace Hopper. [21] The NRP also runs open models for the students of its consortium, and its status page lists the models and the GPUs available: in November 2026 every model on it was an open-weight model, and every one ran on NVIDIA GPUs. [21] As such, an open stack above NVIDIA's processors reaches academic researchers on the hardware that they already use, which is why the outcome of the deals of Section 2 matters to them.

The closest precedent is Android. My earlier research was on Android and its open source project (AOSP), as my research statement describes. Google bought Android in 2005 and released its source as the Android Open Source Project, mostly under the Apache License. [22] Android Market opened with the first Android phone in October 2008, and it is now Google Play. [23] Google agreed in August 2011 to buy Motorola Mobility for $12.5 billion, completed the purchase in May 2012 and sold the company to Lenovo for $2.91 billion in 2014. [24] NVIDIA's moves have the same shape: it has agreed to buy the hub, Hugging Face, licensed the system that builds Poolside's models and invested in Reflection AI, and with them it is building an open marketplace for models above its processors. Each sponsor opens the layer that brings it more buyers: Google wanted more phones running its search and its store, and NVIDIA wants more models running on its processors.

Each of these markets has room for a closed and an open platform, as the appendix shows for the first two. [25]

Market Closed Open
Phones iPhone (Apple) Android, open source as AOSP; Google runs its marketplace
Computers and servers Windows (Microsoft) Linux
AI models OpenAI, Anthropic the open ecosystem that NVIDIA finances: Nemotron, Hugging Face (pending), Poolside, Reflection AI

Our hope is that these deals meet no regulatory obstacle. The Department of Justice is reported to be examining the acquisition of Hugging Face. [8] As the case of the NRP shows for my own research, an open ecosystem is where academic research does its long horizon work: open weights, an open hub and open runtimes are what a university lab can study, change and build on for years. The deals of Section 2 are an investment in those layers, so an obstacle to the deals is, for such a lab, a risk to the supply of what it works on.

6. What this means for verification

By the Jevons paradox, the verification of building blocks becomes the key. The paradox is that when a resource is used more efficiently, its total consumption can rise, because cheaper use finds new uses. For tokens as a whole, the appendix gives a measure: the price of a token at a fixed level of capability fell about 200X in two years, while the tokens that one large provider processes rose about 330X. These figures cover every use of the models and not code alone. For code, as for proofs in Mathlib, we have no separate measure, and our reading is that the same is happening there: as proofs and programs become cheaper to write, more of both are written, and each new proof or program rests on the libraries and the runtimes below it. The verification of those building blocks therefore matters most.

The combination of OpenShell and Cedar from AWS may be the key to building a foundational platform for verification: OpenShell confines what an agent may do, and Cedar states the policy in a language that has a formal model.

Part 1's rule sorts the layers. The rule is that a layer which connects independent systems must be open, and that a leaf may be closed provided that the layers around it emit portable evidence. Section 4 adds one qualifier: the connective layer must be open by design and not by promise. For the hub, that means open formats for weights and datasets, downloads that can be reproduced and compared by hash, and records of provenance that a third party can keep.

A verifier can read what is open by design, and no further. In cuTile Rust, the kernel's source and its lowering to Tile IR are Rust that can be read, so the lowering can be audited. A theorem about the source reaches the bytecode only with an argument that the lowering preserves its meaning, or with a check of each translation. The steps after that, tileiras and the driver, are closed. Testing them from outside shows their behaviour on the cases that were run, and it does not extend a proof. What remains possible with a closed compiler is to check one compiled output against the semantics of the machine. Documentation and source can also name the path differently: grout's README describes a path from Rust through MLIR to PTX, while the source at the commit we read lowers to Tile IR, a representation that is itself built on MLIR, and calls tileiras. Only the source therefore shows where the open path ends. Figure F9, from our essay Gang of four: C++, Rust, Lean and Mojo, draws both routes in its Rust band: cuda-oxide to PTX, and cuTile Rust to Tile IR. OpenShell's policies are files that can be compared with what an agent did.

The record must not depend on one vendor. A record that names a model by the hash of its weights, a kernel by its source and bytecode, and a policy by its file can be checked by anyone, provided that it also carries the property that was checked and the proof or the test inputs: the hash identifies what to check, and the rest allows the check to be repeated. A record that says only that a vendor's toolchain accepted the program cannot. We conclude that the open layers of this stack can supply the first kind of record down to the closed base, and that the missing piece is evidence from the closed steps in a form that a third party can check.
