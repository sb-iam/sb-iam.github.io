Summer 2026: Mojo 1.0, DeepSeek Harness, Lean FRO, and the Open Weights Letter

Open and closed systems will coexist. The question is which layers must be open for software written by agents to be safe.

Software is heterogeneous by construction: it spans languages, type systems, intermediate representations, runtimes, protocols, and deployment targets. AI coding agents do not simplify that landscape; instead, they move across it at machine speed, and compositional correctness therefore becomes a systems problem. As such, a software stack native to AI has to return to foundations: type theory for explicit contracts and invariants, category theory for composing tools and effects, and an open ecosystem in which critical interfaces can be inspected, extended, and independently checked.

Four developments in the summer of 2026 made the emerging architecture concrete, because each of them concerns one layer of that stack. Together, they point to four pillars: open models, typed agent harnesses, open compilers, and proof infrastructure that can be checked independently. The four developments were the following:

  • an industry coalition published an open letter on open weights;
  • DeepSeek formalized the agent harness;
  • Modular open sourced the compiler of Mojo, the first general purpose systems language built natively on MLIR;
  • Amazon made the largest gift in the Lean FRO's history, to focus on CSLib, the Mathlib of computer science theory.

Four names in this list recur throughout the essay, and we define them here. An agent harness is the program that runs a model in a loop, gives it tools under stated permissions, and records what it did. MLIR is the Multi-Level Intermediate Representation, the compiler infrastructure of the LLVM project on which Mojo is built. The Lean FRO is the Lean Focused Research Organization, which develops the Lean proof assistant, and CSLib is the Lean Computer Science Library.

Our attitude is optimism about open and closed work alike, and we are exact about layers. Tan and colleagues argued at ICML 2026, in If open source is to win, it must go public, that "open source weights alone are inert" without the layers that activate them, and they conceded that public AI "is not about replacing private labs." The paper uses the phrase "activation gap" once, in its discussion of one project, the National Deep Inference Fabric (NDIF); we borrow the phrase in this essay for the general distance between published weights and a system that can use them. Their question is which layers must be public, whereas ours is which layers must be open for the work of an agent to be checked from outside the vendor.

Figure F0 places the four developments at the end of a sequence of four steps, which we describe below it.

Four steps led to this point, and each of them changed the unit of work that a model produces.

  • November 2022: ChatGPT. The unit of work became an answer.
  • Winter 2024 to 2025: reasoning models and the DeepSeek moment. OpenAI's o1 and the previews of o3 arrived in the same weeks as DeepSeek's V3 and R1, both of which have open weights; the unit of work became a chain of reasoning, and the weights could come from any laboratory.
  • Fall 2025: coding agents everywhere, and OpenClaw begins. Claude Code, Codex CLI and Cursor, together with the open personal agent of Peter Steinberger, made the unit of work an action on a repository.
  • Summer 2026: an open ecosystem for AI. An ecosystem began to open around the agent, so that the unit of work can be a change that someone else can check to be safe.

The rest of this essay is organized as follows. Section 1 describes the layers of the industry and the finer layers that matter for agents, and Section 2 states the scope of the series. Section 3 gives the principles under which development by agents becomes verifiable, and Section 4 describes the events of the summer layer by layer. Finally, Section 5 explains why private firms open a layer, and Section 6 describes the tooling on which the ecosystem depends.

1. The Open AI Layer Cake

This section describes the layers of the AI industry as Jensen Huang presents them, and then the finer layers into which the top of that stack divides for coding agents. We begin with Huang's description because it describes the industry as it is built. "Industrially, AI is actually essentially a five-layer cake," he said at Davos on 21 January 2026. His post of 10 March 2026, "AI Is a 5-Layer Cake", names the layers from bottom to top as energy, chips, infrastructure, models and applications, and it adds that "every successful application pulls on every layer beneath it, all the way down to the power plant that keeps it alive."

The two bottom layers, energy and chips, decide the lowest cost at which the layers above them can be offered. This series covers the top three layers, applications, models and infrastructure, because the code is written in the first two and the agent runs in the third; the runtime and sandbox of an agent, such as OpenShell, are infrastructure. Chips and energy, custom chips included, are therefore out of scope. Figure F8 marks this boundary on Huang's cake, and Figure F3 shows how, for agents, the three layers in scope divide further.

The division of Figure F3 is the activation gap, drawn for one domain. That domain is the writing of software by agents. Each layer commits what the layer above left informal, in a form that the layer below can check, and each commitment is either open to inspection or held inside one vendor. The six layers are the following:

  • Human intent commits a goal and a person who accepts the risk. It arrives through whichever client that person uses, and that client may be open or closed.
  • Formal intent and policy commits the task boundary: what the agent may touch, what it must preserve, and what counts as done. Cedar, an open language for authorization policies, is the open reference for authority, and Lean and CSLib are where proof obligations are stated and discharged. This layer must be open, because a policy whose semantics only one product implements cannot be checked by a second tool, and a proof that only one vendor can check remains, for everyone else, an unverified claim.
  • The agent harness commits execution: which tool ran, under which capability, with what result, and whether the run can be replayed. Its interface must be open, that is, the Agent Client Protocol (ACP) through which the editor communicates with the agent, and the log of what the model saw. The implementation can be anyone's, and DeepSeek Harness opened this layer in August.
  • Compiler and language commits meaning: typed structure, an intermediate representation (IR) to which analyzers can attach, and hooks for checks that run before lowering erases the information they need. The IR and its analyzers must be open, whereas the compiler that produces them can be open or closed. Modular open sourced the compiler of Mojo at ModCon.
  • The executable system and its runtime commit behavior: what runs, where it runs, and the traces that it produces. The runtime, for example the OpenShell sandbox, is infrastructure. This layer may be open or closed.
  • Evidence and witness commits the record: what was proved, refuted, tested, observed, judged, assumed, or never checked, in a format that a second tool can read. It must be portable, because evidence that can be read only in the dashboard of one vendor cannot be checked again by anyone else.

The weights are what these layers activate, and they may be open or closed. Below them are chips and energy, which are outside this series. As such, a flagship model with open weights lowers the cost at which the layers above it can run, but it establishes nothing about whether the software that an agent writes is safe.

We refer to the position that this thread argues for as the Goldilocks zone. It is a calibration on two axes, which Figure F4 draws.

  • Rigor runs from intent that is stated only in a prompt, with ambient permissions, to a proof for every edit; the zone scales the evidence that is required to the risk of the change.
  • Openness runs from one vendor that owns every layer to scattered source code that never composes; the zone opens the connective layers, whereas a leaf, by which we mean a layer through which no other layer communicates, may be open or closed.

Next, Section 2 states which of these layers the series covers.

2. What this series covers

This section states the scope of the series, first what it covers and then what it leaves out.

In scope: coding.

  • The layers of Figure F3 and the software engineering that runs through them.
  • Typed intent and policy.
  • Agent harnesses, and the protocols between editor, agent, and tool. We covered the three dimensions of these protocols, ACP, the Model Context Protocol (MCP) and the Agent2Agent protocol (A2A), in an earlier talk.
  • Compilers, languages, and the IRs to which analyzers attach.
  • The executable system, its runtime, and its traces.
  • Evidence, and the formats in which it travels.
  • The agentic software development loop, as the subject that is being made safe.
  • Open models, weights, and reinforcement learning (RL) environments, exactly where they touch those layers: what runs locally, what a reward can verify, and what a license allows.

Out of scope.

  • The ethics of open versus closed. The series takes coexistence as given and argues only about layers.
  • Chips, custom chips included, energy, physical hardware, and robotics. They decide the cost floor. For the safety of software, the series takes correct hardware as given, which is also an assumption of the proof of seL4.
  • Anything outside software engineering.

Several threads are evolving alongside this one, and we will cover them across the essays of the series: the Goldilocks zone of the open software ecosystem, hybrid analysis, and the future of software engineering, among others. Next, Section 3 describes the development loop that the layers in scope are meant to make verifiable.

3. Software development assisted by AI, made verifiable

This section describes the development loop that agents now run and the three principles under which its output can be verified. Figure F6 shows the loop with the layers of Section 1 placed around it.

The loop itself has not changed, but every step in it now runs faster. SWE-agent and OpenHands run it at the scale of a repository, and the literature refers to the result as the agentic software development life cycle (SDLC). A systematic review of 92 studies identified the factor that decides adoption: "output verifiability is the primary enabler of agentic adoption." As such, the question for this section is how the output of the loop becomes verifiable.

A verifiable ecosystem lets agents build verified software at large scale on three principles that the functional programming and hardware communities already apply.

  • The formal spec is the contract. RISC-V specifies its architecture twice: the manual of the instruction set architecture (ISA) is the official prose, and the Sail model is the official formal specification. The Architectural Certification Tests compute expected results from Sail and gate the "RISC-V Compatible" mark. The analogue for agents is that intent is written in a specification that tools can execute, while the prose remains a description for people.
  • Make illegal states unrepresentable. The maxim of Yaron Minsky is the working rule of typed functional programming, and the best known verified systems follow it: CompCert is written in the Rocq proof assistant (formerly Coq) and extracted to OCaml; the 8,700 lines of C in seL4 were derived from a Haskell prototype and proved correct in 200,000 lines of Isabelle; and QuickCheck turned properties into executable tests. As such, an agent that cannot express a forbidden state cannot reach it.
  • Verification is the sign off, not an afterthought. Verification consumes 60 to 70 percent of the engineering effort on a chip project; simulation still accounts for most of that effort, and the use of formal methods grows every year. The authors of Csmith, a random generator of C programs, spent about six CPU years without finding a bug that produced wrong code in the verified middle end of CompCert, and they still concluded that "verification does not obviate testing, but rather complements it." Proofs and tests are therefore used together, and neither is sufficient alone for a change to be merged.

Before AI, these principles were learned by hand. Software Foundations, the series of textbooks edited by Benjamin C. Pierce in which every definition and proof is checked by the Rocq proof assistant (formerly Coq), taught programmers to state a specification and to prove a program against it. Agents now need the same discipline at machine speed.

Hardware is released only when its verification has signed off, and we argue that software written by agents should be released on the same condition. Next, Section 4 describes the events of the summer that gave these layers open references.

4. Summer 2026, layer by layer

This section describes the four events of the summer and the layers that they concern. Figure F7 places each event on its layer and states its contribution.

The four events fell within four weeks, from 24 July to 18 August 2026. They concern four layers, and each event made one contribution:

  • the letter put the industry on record for open weights;
  • the gift from Amazon funds the proof checker at an independent organization, the Lean FRO;
  • DeepSeek Harness gives the agent harness a formal basis, by way of Cordis;
  • the compiler of Mojo, the first general purpose systems language built natively on MLIR, is now open source.

The subsections below take five layers in turn: the four layers of these events and a fifth, models and environments, which also acquired open references this year. The five layers lie on both sides of the line between what must be open and what may be closed.

Compiler: Mojo goes open at ModCon

  • Mojo 1.0 was released on August 11 as a stability milestone: most core features of the language are stable at the source level, the standard library begins with a small set of stable APIs, the 1.x series follows the rule that "changes should primarily be additive," although breaking changes remain possible, and the application binary interface (ABI) is not yet stable.
  • A week later at ModCon, Modular open sourced the entire compiler and toolchain under Apache 2.0 with LLVM exceptions.
  • The runtime of MAX is planned to be open source by the end of the year, and Modular is now a Qualcomm company.

Modular's earlier documentation called Mojo "the first major language designed expressly for MLIR", and its current vision document says that Mojo is "built purely on top of MLIR Core" and aims at "a unified language for kernel development." Our argument, which Modular does not make, is that an open compiler for a general purpose language built directly on MLIR is a worked reference for two kinds of later work: domain specific languages (DSLs) built on MLIR, and verification hooks that run before lowering. For the language itself, we refer the reader to Modular's post on Mojo 1.0.

Modular describes Mojo as the systems language for the AI era, and we add to that description the part that Modular does not state:

  • First on MLIR, first for the AI era. Mojo is the first general purpose systems language built natively on MLIR and the first mainstream language built for the AI era, and its target is the data plane, that is, both training and inference. The first of these two descriptions is our own statement, and it follows the earlier wording of Modular quoted above.
  • Heterogeneous kernels. Mojo compiles one kernel source for heterogeneous hardware, that is, for CPUs and for GPUs from more than one vendor. The GPU kernels of MAX, the graph compiler and serving runtime of Modular, are written in Mojo; they and the Python API of MAX are licensed under Apache 2.0, and its runtime is planned to be open source by the end of the year.
  • One stack for inference and training is planned. At present a model is trained in PyTorch or JAX and is served by a separate engine such as vLLM or SGLang, so that it crosses a language boundary on the way. MAX serves inference only; training in MAX is planned, and until then training in Mojo is a community effort (llm.mojo, Nabla, and our own nabla-mojo).

We can leverage fifty years of program analysis on one platform when training and inference share it, because no boundary between languages remains at which an analysis loses the program. Performance is an additional benefit: the platform brings a systems language with speed in the class of Rust and C++ and with abstractions at zero cost.

Modular does not yet accept contributions to the compiler, "particularly in today's era of AI coding," and it aims to accept them by the end of the year; the compiler is therefore open in its source and closed in its governance. This limit does not hinder an analyzer, because an analyzer needs the IR to be open and does not need access to the queue of contributions, which may stay closed. Next, we describe the harness.

Harness: DeepSeek Harness on Cordis

DeepSeek Harness, whose binary is named dsh, was released in August as a developer preview under the MIT license. It is built on Cordis, a plugin framework from 2022 that it credits as its foundation, and it maintains two invariants:

  • Everything is a plugin, the agent loop included, with no privileged core.
  • What the model sees is logged: anything that reaches a model request must be reconstructable from the session log, to which entries are only ever appended.

The coding agent pi, by Mario Zechner, gives the opposite answer to the same problem:

  • pi keeps a core of four tools and declines subagents, a plan mode, and the Model Context Protocol (MCP);
  • dsh provides subagents, planning, skills, sandboxing, and an approval policy;
  • only the Minimal preset of dsh, which at release was a loop of two tools for benchmarking, resembles pi, and the only trace of pi in dsh is one npm package behind one model adapter plugin.

The main contribution is a formalization of the agent harness. The formal treatment is in the paper on Cordis by Shi, Zhang and Cui, which names the agent harness that evolves itself as a motivating case and formalizes two properties of dynamic composition. The first property is that of revertible effects: every change that a component makes to the shared context carries an inverse that the runtime holds, so that removing the component undoes the changes that it made through that context. The second property is that of reactive coeffects: every change to the context is checked against the declared dependencies of each component, and the component is activated or deactivated accordingly. A calculus of components then carries both properties from one component to a whole system.

Note that the paper does not name DeepSeek Harness, and its case study is Koishi, another system built on Cordis. The statement that the agent harness is formalized is therefore our reading, and it rests on one fact: DeepSeek Harness is built on Cordis, so the two properties formalized for the framework are the ones on which the composition of the harness relies. The result covers the plugin composition of the harness, that is, the changes made through the tracked context. It does not cover an effect outside that context, such as a file written by a subprocess, nor the reconstruction of a model request from the log, nor the correctness of the agent loop. The paper appeared in late August, after this essay was first published, and it was added in revision. Figure F9 redraws Table 2 of the paper, which pairs each construct of the theory with the code of Cordis that implements it.

Tan and colleagues describe this layer as the one in which a proprietary harness "captures the user's exact process of software development," and they also note that open harnesses exist at a higher price. Our rule for the layer is that the log and the plugin interface must be open, while the harness itself can be anyone's; in this case a model laboratory has opened all three. Next, we describe the proof layer.

Proofs: Amazon funds Lean outside Amazon; CSLib builds the library

On July 26 the Automated Reasoning organization of Amazon made what the Lean FRO calls the single largest donation in its history, to a body outside Amazon; the amount was not disclosed. Amazon gave the argument for open proof tools as its reason: a proof is easier to accept when its reader can evaluate the tools behind it.

CSLib is the place where we expect the gift to matter most for computer science, although it is a separate initiative. Note that the post by Amazon does not name CSLib; we connect the two because Amazon is among the institutions that steer the library and because the library is written in Lean, whose development the gift funds. CSLib has the following characteristics:

  • it was announced in August 2025 and described in a white paper in February 2026: it is a counterpart of Mathlib for computer science, together with Boole, an intermediate language in the style of Boogie whose verification conditions are discharged in Lean;
  • it is steered by Amazon, Google DeepMind, Stanford, UT Austin, Southern Denmark, and the Lean FRO, and it is funded through a program of Renaissance Philanthropy;
  • it is explicit about the constraint that it addresses, "AI-based provers are bottlenecked by the abstractions available in the underlying proof assistant," and, in our judgment, it is right about governance: AI has an advisory role only, and every commit passes through manual review.

In our judgment, a checker that is governed independently, together with a shared library of abstractions, is the largest step that formal verification aided by AI has taken toward agents that write proofs which humans can check. The safety of every proof rests on the checker, and its governance must therefore be open. Next, we describe the letter on open weights.

Weights: the coalition letter

On July 24 NVIDIA hosted "Open Weights and American AI Leadership," an open letter to policymakers in the United States. Three facts about the letter matter for our argument:

  • It grew from 25 signatories at launch, led by NVIDIA, Microsoft, and Meta, to 235 in its revision of July 29; Google, OpenAI, and Amazon joined within the week, and most signatories are startups, infrastructure vendors, and nonprofits.
  • It is advocacy and not a commitment, and it rests on five arguments: access, competition, control, adaptability, and security.
  • The last argument reverses the usual position on security: "Relying solely on closed models is not inherently safe."

The letter argues for the artifact, that is, the weights, whereas Tan and colleagues describe the same artifact as inert without the layers around it. We consider both positions correct: weights set the cost floor and are a leaf whether they are open or closed, and the layers above them decide whether the output is safe. Next, we describe the models and the environments in which they are trained and scored.

Models and environments: Prime Intellect, GLM, Kimi, and a box on the desk

The stack of Prime Intellect separates the trainer from the task, in the following parts:

  • prime-rl (Apache 2.0) runs the asynchronous RL loop.
  • verifiers (MIT) packages a task as an environment: a dataset, a harness and reward functions.
  • The Environments Hub holds more than 2,500 environments under version control.
  • The reward is a deterministic check, or the judgment of a large language model (LLM) "where deterministic evaluation is impractical." The following mapping is ours and not Prime Intellect's: a deterministic reward is a form of verification, a judged reward is a form of validation, and one environment scores both training and evaluation.

The open models, and the desktop system on which some of them run, are as follows:

  • GLM-5.2 by Z.ai (753B parameters, MIT) and Kimi K3 by Moonshot (2.8T parameters, Kimi K3 License) approach the frontier models on the benchmarks of their own laboratories; Moonshot itself says that K3 "still trails the most powerful proprietary models."
  • Neither model runs on a DGX Spark. NVIDIA rates that system, which costs $4,699 and has 128 GB of memory, for inference on models of up to 200B parameters and for fine tuning of models of up to 70B.
  • The models that fit are GLM-4.7-Flash (30B-A3B, MIT) and its peers of 30B to 70B parameters. NVIDIA documents fine tuning with LoRA on the system, whereas running the RL loop of prime-rl on it is not documented.

In the terms of Section 1, models are leaves and environments are evidence. A reward that nobody else can run cannot serve as a benchmark, and environments must therefore be open. Next, Section 5 explains why private firms open a layer at all.

5. Why open? Four incentives, one boundary

This section explains why private firms open a layer, and it then states the rule that we use to decide which layers must be open. The five layers of Section 4 share one mechanism, and that mechanism is not a belief that open source is good in itself. Private actors open a layer for one of four reasons, and the reasons are not mutually exclusive.

  • Common infrastructure. "This should be something everyone can build on." Internet standards, Linux and LLVM show that a foundational layer is worth more when it is shared than when one firm holds it.
  • Strategic collaboration. "We benefit more by sharing this layer than by competing over it." The motive may be shared cost, catching up to an incumbent, commoditizing a layer that is not core, or competing somewhere else. A company opens the layer in which the growth of the ecosystem is worth more to it than exclusivity, and it captures value in a layer next to it.
  • Network effects. "The technology becomes more valuable when everyone can participate." Programming languages, compilers and IRs, protocols, package ecosystems, agent interfaces and policy languages are each worth more with every implementation that supports them, and closing one removes the externality that made it valuable.
  • Community for the challenger. "We are not at the leading edge, so we open our work and let a community grow around it." A company that is behind a closed leader opens its work so that developers and customers gather around it and not around the closed product; the Llama series of models from Meta is the example.

LLVM is an example of the first three at once, which is why the series Democratizing AI Compute by Chris Lattner is the closest precedent for this one:

  • as common infrastructure, it is a compiler substrate under a permissive license;
  • as collaboration, it lets Apple, Google, NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel share a large body of compiler engineering rather than build it five times;
  • as a network, it is worth more with every language, target, and tool that connects to it.

Open ecosystems emerge when private actors find more value in sharing a foundational layer than in owning it.

In some cases the opening begins as a public good, in others as a strategic necessity, and in others because the technology cannot reach its value without a network; in most cases all three come to reinforce one another. The sentence above recurs through the thread, and it sharpens the question that the series asks: which layers of the AI software stack become common infrastructure, which stay competitive, and what economic or technical force draws the line? The question leaves room to read Linux, LLVM and MLIR, Mojo, Rust, Cedar, ACP and MCP, agent harnesses, models, and inference runtimes each on its own terms, without the assumption that they are open for the same reason.

Tan and colleagues answer this question with institutions, whereas we answer it with a rule in two parts.

  • A layer that exists to connect independent systems must be open: policy semantics, agent protocols, effect logs, compiler IR and its analyzers, evidence formats, the proof checker.
  • A leaf can be closed, as long as the layers around it emit portable evidence: weights, hosted inference, the best client, a compiler's contribution queue.

The test that a reader can apply is whether the value of a layer comes from connecting things that do not share an owner. For example, Zed passes the test, because it is an editor under GPL 3.0 that uses a protocol under Apache 2.0 to communicate with closed agents. A harness whose session log only its vendor can parse, on the other hand, does not pass it. Next, Section 6 applies the same rule to the tools that an AI engineer uses today.

6. The ecosystem is its tooling

This section describes the tooling on which an open ecosystem depends, and it shows where the boundary between open and closed lies in the tools of today.

An ecosystem is as good as its developer tooling. Weights without a harness cannot act on a repository, and a proof without an open checker rests on the assurance of one vendor. As such, safety comes from the compiler that checks, the harness that logs, the prover that admits a proof, and the evidence format that survives a change of vendor.

The table below lists the AI stack layer by layer, from the models up to policy and proofs, and it already shows the boundary:

Layer Open Closed, hosted
Models GLM-5.2 (MIT) · Kimi K3 (Kimi K3 License) · Llama (Meta) GPT (OpenAI) · Claude (Anthropic) · Gemini (Google)
Training PyTorch (2016, BSD 3-Clause, PyTorch Foundation) · JAX (Apache 2.0) none
RL post training and environments prime-rl · verifiers (Prime Intellect) · verl (ByteDance) · TRL (Hugging Face) · OpenRLHF Fireworks reinforcement fine tuning
Inference vLLM · SGLang (LMSYS) Fireworks · every frontier API
Compiler and language Mojo (Apache 2.0 with LLVM exceptions) · LLVM and MLIR the runtime of MAX (planned to be open source by the end of the year)
Runtime and sandbox OpenShell the hosted sandbox of each vendor
Agent harness and orchestration LangChain / LangGraph · OpenAI Agents SDK · DeepSeek Harness Claude Code (public repository; its license reserves all rights) · Cursor
Agent protocols ACP (Apache 2.0) · MCP none
Policy and proofs Cedar · Lean · CSLib none

The open column holds every connective layer of the table, and the closed column holds only leaves. Our reading is that a team can therefore run the whole loop with the tools of the open column today, and that the tools of the closed column compete by being better and not by being the only way in.
